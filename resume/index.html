    Cake by VPBank · AI PRODUCT STRATEGY · TEAM ENABLEMENT

    Advance AI-first banking and the product team’s operating model

    Turn an AI-first banking strategy into useful customer products and a faster product operating model.

    The production text/image transfer feature achieved approximately 80% correct prefilling. I enabled 10+ PMs to use AI-assisted prototyping and automation, reducing PM preparation effort by roughly 50%; prototypes that previously took hours could be prepared in about 30 minutes. Voice banking remained a research/prototype track.

    What made this hard

    Cake wanted AI to improve operating performance and make its AI-first banking proposition tangible. I split that direction into two tracks: customer-facing banking capabilities and internal PM productivity. Chat-based commerce exposed an everyday customer problem, while our lean product team spent hours turning requirements into prototypes, documentation, and tickets.

    The choice I made

    I prioritized AI use cases by frequency, effort saved, the consequence of a wrong answer, and whether banking services could validate the result. Text/image transfer drafting could solve a defined task while retaining customer authorization. Voice banking needed a longer research/prototype roadmap. Internally, I chose reusable workflows grounded in the PM’s product context rather than treating AI output as finished requirements.

    What I did

    1. Translated strategy into two roadmaps

      Defined a customer-product track for transfer assistance and voice-banking exploration, alongside an internal track for prototypes, PRDs, tickets, and product coordination. Kept production capabilities distinct from research.

    2. Started from an everyday behavior

      Customers receive payment details in chats and screenshots. I framed the product around removing re-entry, rather than adding an open-ended banking chatbot.

    3. Drew the AI boundary

      Separated input interpretation and transaction drafting from banking validation and customer authorization. Voice banking stayed on a research/prototype track.

    4. Made prototypes reviewable

      Supplied flows, component rules, typography, and interaction logic to AI-assisted prototyping; brought working drafts to engineers for refinement.

    5. Scaled the method across the team

      Created PRD and ticket workflows using PM-supplied context, including Jira publishing through MCP. Trained 10+ PMs; prototypes that took hours could be prepared in roughly 30 minutes.

    What I took from it

    My contribution was both product and organizational: decide where AI creates useful leverage, define its boundaries, and equip the team to use it. The roughly 50% improvement describes PM preparation effort across AI-assisted tasks, not an independently measured increase in engineering throughput.

    Fundiin · APPLIED ML · RISK OPERATIONS

    Scale risk screening with ML and human judgment

    Make ML useful inside a risk decision system, with the right evaluation and human boundaries.

    ML-based screening reduced manual-review workload by 50% across a $2M monthly lending portfolio. Data Science owned model development; my contribution connected business framing, evaluation, workflow design, and deployment.

    What made this hard

    Fundiin’s rule-based screening was becoming harder to maintain, and Risk Operations still carried a substantial manual-review workload. The opportunity was to use combinations of transaction, merchant, device, behavioral, and customer signals more effectively. Overall accuracy would not be a sufficient launch criterion: rare fraud creates class imbalance, and false positives can reject good customers while missed fraud creates real losses.

    The choice I made

    I brought Risk, Data Science, and Engineering together around the operating outcome and the economics of errors. We defined the model’s role, policy constraints, thresholds, and manual-review bands. With limited data-science capacity, we selected AutoML for the initial production iteration so the team could learn from the decision workflow without first building a custom training stack. The tradeoff was less transparency and control, making validation and oversight especially important.

    What I did

    1. Aligned on the cost of errors

      Brought Risk, Data Science, and Engineering together around prediction targets, precision/coverage tradeoffs, and the customer and financial costs of wrong decisions.

    2. Chose speed to learning over a custom stack

      With limited data-science capacity, selected AutoML for the first iteration. Data Science owned model training; I connected business features, policy constraints, and the operating workflow.

    3. Challenged misleading offline performance

      Asked whether proposed signals would have existed at the original decision time, surfacing potential information leakage without treating later-known outcomes as usable inputs.

    4. Validated before changing customer decisions

      Ran alongside the existing process in shadow mode for roughly two to three months, inspected disagreements, then staged rollout with human review for uncertain cases.

    What I took from it

    The product is the complete decision system: model, policy rules, thresholds, exceptions, monitoring, and human accountability. The resume’s 70% figure describes coverage of expert-identified rejection cases, not a claim of 70% model precision. Data Science owned model training; I connected evaluation and the operating workflow to the business outcome.

    ZaloPay · VNG · CUSTOMER DISCOVERY · EXPERIMENTATION

    Find the real payment friction through data and customer interviews

    Use customer research to challenge the funnel hypothesis, then experiment on the right problem.

    The work improved payment conversion by 20% through instrumentation fixes, customer insight, and 10 A/B-tested funnel experiments.

    What made this hard

    The payment journey lost users between intent and completion. Looking only at aggregate conversion hid different causes: incomplete instrumentation, errors in partner-specific flows, and genuine customer confusion. A drop on a chart told us where to investigate, but did not explain what needed to change.

    The choice I made

    I started with SQL and Python to repair missing events and segment the funnel by partner, interaction step, device, and failure behavior. I paired that analysis with more than 20 customer interviews and usability testing. One important finding overturned our initial belief that layout was causing a drop: customer conversations and segmented behavior pointed to intermittent technical failures later in the journey on a wider range of devices. Reliability became the priority before another screen redesign.

    What I did

    1. Fixed the measurement before optimizing

      Found incomplete instrumentation and partner-flow errors. Added missing events and segmented the funnel by partner, step, device, and failure behavior with SQL and Python.

    2. Used research to test the explanation

      Combined 20+ customer interviews with usability testing. A drop-off chart could locate a problem, but customer accounts and observed behavior helped explain it.

    3. Abandoned the layout hypothesis

      Evidence pointed to intermittent downstream failures across a wider range of devices. I redirected attention to reliability before asking for another screen redesign.

    4. Experimented after diagnosing the blockers

      Led 10 A/B-tested changes across content, information hierarchy, CTA placement, button treatment, and flow simplification, contributing to the 20% conversion improvement.

    What I took from it

    Research creates value when it changes the decision. Combining quantitative and qualitative evidence helped us separate missing measurement, technical failure, and customer choice—and stop optimizing the wrong explanation.
